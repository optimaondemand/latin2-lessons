📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Yesterday you met the idea. Today you build the form — and the good news is you already know the endings.
The PPP has exactly two moving parts: a stem taken from the fourth principal part, and the 2–1–2 adjective endings you learned back in Latin 1 (magnus, magna, magnum). Learn the two steps here and you can form the PPP of any regular verb in the language.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to find the PPP stem by dropping -um from the fourth principal part.
2
I will be able to add 2–1–2 adjective endings to build the PPP in the right gender, number, and case.
3
I will be able to explain why one PPP stem can appear in dozens of different forms across a text.

📚 PART 2 · STEP ONE: THE STEM 10 min

Strip the fourth principal part

Take the fourth principal part and drop the -um. What is left is the PPP stem. Then add the 2–1–2 endings -us / -a / -um for masculine, feminine, and neuter.

Verb4th partPPP (m. / f. / n.)Meaning

Notice: some fourth parts look nothing like the present stem (mittō → miss-, vincō → vict-). This is exactly why you memorize all four principal parts — the fourth is unpredictable, and it is the one the PPP needs.

📌 PART 3 · STEP TWO: DECLINE IT 10 min

The PPP declines like magnus, -a, -um

Because the PPP is a 2–1–2 adjective, it takes the full set of adjective endings. Here is amātus, amāta, amātum in the four core cases, singular:

CaseMasc. (amāt-)Fem. (amāt-)Neut. (amāt-)
Nom.amātusamātaamātum
Gen.amātīamātaeamātī
Acc.amātumamātamamātum
Abl.amātōamātāamātō
💡 RULE 1 & RULE 2

Rule 1 — the source. Strip -um from the fourth part; add 2–1–2 endings. amātum → amāt- → amātus / amāta / amātum.

Rule 2 — agreement. Like any adjective, the PPP takes the gender, number, and case of the noun it describes. So one stem can appear in dozens of forms: velum cruentum (n. sg. acc.), Thisbē vulnerāta (f. sg. nom.), folia mīssa (n. pl. nom.).
