📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
For seven modules you built the passive verb. This week you take that action off the verb line and turn it into an adjective.
In Module 07 you left Pyramus and Thisbe at the moment of crisis — Thisbe fled the spring, her veil left behind. This module witnesses the tragedy: the lion at the spring, the bloodstained veil, the wounded lovers, the transformed fruit. Notice the English words — bloodstained, wounded, transformed. Each is a completed action turned into a describing word. That is exactly what Latin's Perfect Passive Participle (PPP) does. Today there is nothing to drill — just one big idea to take in.

⏰ PART 1 · WARM-UP 5 min

What you can already do with the passive

You have spent seven modules building the passive voice as a verb: something is being done to the subject. Read these two — you already understand them.

Velum laniātur.

The veil is being torn. (present passive — the action is happening now)

Thisbē terrētur.

Thisbe is being frightened. (present passive)

💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · no wrong answer
Those are passive verbs — the action is in progress. But English also says “the torn veil.” What has changed about “torn” there?
📚 PART 2 · THE BIG IDEA 12 min

From doing to describing

A passive verb reports an action being done. A Perfect Passive Participle takes that same passive action, marks it as complete, and turns it into an adjective that describes a noun.

Passive verb — action in progress

Velum laniātur.
The veil is being torn.

Reports an event happening now.

PPP — a describing word

velum cruentum
the bloodstained veil

Describes the veil by a completed action.

This is the grammar of evidence. When Pyramus finds Thisbe's veil torn and bloodied, he is not watching an action happen — he is reading the aftermath of one. The PPP captures exactly that: an action completed, its result standing before you.

💡 WHERE DOES IT COME FROM?

Every Latin verb has four principal parts, e.g. amō, amāre, amāvī, amātum. You have been memorizing that fourth part since Latin 1. The PPP is built from it: amātum → amātus, amāta, amātum = “loved.” Tomorrow you learn the mechanics; today, just hold the idea.
