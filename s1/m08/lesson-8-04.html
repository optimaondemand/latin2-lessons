⏰ PART 1 · LECTIŌ 18 min

Read the ending of the story

Read aloud, line by line. The bold words are the participles and adjectives that carry the completed action. Use the glossary for the unfamiliar words.

After Ovid, Metamorphōsēs IV — adapted for Latin 2

1Leō magnus ad fontem vēnit et velum Thisbēs ibi invēnit. Thisbē, territa ā leōne, in speluncam fūgerat.

2Leō velum laniat: velum nunc cruentum et lacerum in terrā iacet.

3Pyramus mox ad arborem mōrī vēnit. Velum lacerum et cruentum vīdit — et statim Thisbēn mortuam esse crēdidit.

4Miser Pyramus, vulnerātus amōre, sub arbōre cōnstitit et gladium in pectus suum iēcit.

5Sanguis effūsus frūctūs albōs mōrī tinxit: frūctūs, mūtātī ā sanguine, rubrī factī sunt.

6Thisbē ē speluncā exit et Pyramum moribundum sub arbōre invēnit.

7Thisbē, plōrāns, super corpus Pyramī sē prōiēcit et eōdem gladiō mortua est.

8Frūctūs mōrī, mūtātī ā sanguine amāntium, semper rubrī erunt — in memoriam amōris perditī.

Glossary:  fontem — spring, fountain (acc. sg. of fōns, fontis) · speluncam — cave (acc. sg. of spelunca, -ae) · laniat — tears, rips (pres. act. of laniō) · lacerum — torn (regular adjective, agreeing with velum) · mortuam esse crēdidit — believed (her) to be dead (indirect statement) · pectus — chest, breast (acc. sg.) · effūsus — PPP of effundō, poured out, shed · tinxit — dyed, stained (perf. of tingō) · moribundum — dying (adjective) · plōrāns — weeping (present participle, preview) · amāntium — of the lovers (gen. pl., pres. participle as noun) · perditī — PPP of perdō, lost, destroyed (gen. sg. m., agreeing with amōris)

Commit first: in your notes, translate lines 1–3 and identify each PPP (where present) and the noun it agrees with. Then reveal to compare.

1. “A great lion came to the spring and found Thisbe's veil there. Thisbe, frightened by the lion, had fled into a cave.” — territa = PPP of terreō, f. sg. nom., agreeing with Thisbe.
2. “The lion tears the veil: the veil now lies on the ground, bloodstained and torn.” — cruentum and lacerum are regular adjectives (n. sg.), agreeing with velum.
3. “Pyramus soon came to the mulberry tree. He saw the torn and bloodstained veil — and at once believed Thisbe to be dead.” — lacerum/cruentum = adjectives with velum; Thisbēn mortuam esse = indirect statement.
📚 PART 2 · CULTURAL READING 8 min
🌿 THE MULBERRY TREE · AN AETIOLOGY

Ovid's story is an aetiology — a mythological origin story. Before Pyramus and Thisbe, the mulberry's fruit was white. Stained by the lovers' blood, the tree's fruit turned dark red permanently. The Latin name mōrus appears in Ovid's original; the tree is real — mulberries grew throughout the ancient Mediterranean and were cultivated for silkworms. Roman readers would have recognized the tree at once, and thereafter seen the lovers' grief in every dark berry.
