Lesson 4.03
★ Naming the Doer — Ā/Ab + Ablative
Lesson · about 40 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Today the passive gets its missing half back: the doer.
You learn the agent construction — Ā/ab + the ablative — which names the person or being by whom a passive action is done. You also learn to tell it apart from a look-alike: the ablative of means. Then you meet the module's eight vocabulary words, all of which appear in tomorrow's reading. This is the day the grammar becomes usable.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to form and translate the agent construction, choosing ā before a consonant and ab before a vowel.
2
I will be able to distinguish an ablative of agent (ā/ab + abl.) from an ablative of means (plain ablative).
3
I will be able to recognize and use the module's eight new vocabulary words.
📚 PART 1 · THE RULES 14 min

Three things to know

1. Form: ā/ab + ablative

Use ā before a word beginning with a consonant; use ab before a word beginning with a vowel or h. The ablative noun is the agent — the one performing the action.

Echō ā Narcissō spernībātur.

Echo was being scorned by Narcissus. (ā + consonant)

Vōx Echōnis ab omnibus audībātur.

The voice of Echo was being heard by all. (ab + vowel)

2. Agent vs. means — the critical distinction

The ablative of means or instrument (a thing used to do the action) takes a plain ablative, with no preposition. Only a person or animate being acting intentionally takes ā/ab. When you see a bare ablative without ā/ab, do not call it an agent.

Means (plain abl.)

Narcissus flammā caecā ūrēbātur.
was being burned by a blind flame — a thing, no ā/ab

Agent (ā/ab + abl.)

Narcissus ā deā pūnītus est.
was punished by the goddess — a person, with ā
3. Word order: the agent floats freely

The agent phrase can appear anywhere in the sentence — Latin word order is flexible, so do not assume it sits next to the verb. Find the preposition ā/ab, identify its ablative noun, and connect it to the passive verb.

Ā deā saevā Narcissus mīrābiliter pūniētur.

Narcissus will be punished in a remarkable way by the cruel goddess. (agent phrase up front, verb at the end)

💡 A HEADS-UP ON MĪROR

Mīror is a deponent verb — passive in form, active in meaning. You will study deponents fully in Latin 3. For now, just treat mīrātur as “he marvels at.”

✅ TRY IT · choose the preposition
To say “by the nymph” (nymphā), which form of the preposition do you use?

In your own words: why does flammā (“by a flame”) take no preposition, while ā deā (“by the goddess”) does? Say your reasoning, then reveal.

A flame is a thing — an instrument or means — so it takes the plain ablative with no preposition. A goddess is a person / animate being acting intentionally, so she is an agent and takes ā/ab + ablative. Only living, willing doers get ā/ab.
💡 PART 4 · TRY BEFORE YOU LOOK 3 min

Translate and label

In your notes, translate each sentence and label the underlined phrase as agent or means. Commit before you reveal.

1. Echō ā Narcissō spernībātur semper.
2. Corpus aquā limpidā vīsum est.
3. Imāgō ab ipsō Narcissō amābātur.
1. “Echo was always being scorned by Narcissus.” — Agent (ā + ablative of a person).
2. “The body was seen by/with clear water.” — Means (plain ablative, no ā/ab; water is an instrument).
3. “The image was being loved by Narcissus himself.” — Agent (ab before a vowel; ipse intensifies “by Narcissus himself”).
