📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Every passive verb raises one question — by whom? This week Latin gives you the tool to answer it.
In Module 03 you learned how the passive voice works in the present system for the first three conjugations. Now you complete the picture. This module adds the future passive for the 3rd and 4th conjugations, and — more importantly — the construction that names the doer the passive hides: the agent construction, ā/ab + ablative. There is nothing to drill today. Today you meet the story that makes all of it matter: Ovid's Narcissus et Echō.

1
I will be able to conjugate 3rd and 4th conjugation verbs in the future passive, and tell the two apart.
2

⏰ PART 1 · WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW 6 min

The passive, recalled

The passive voice flips a sentence around. Instead of the subject doing the action, the subject receives it. You have already met this in the present system. Read these two — both are Latin you can handle right now.

Narcissus nympham spernit.

Narcissus scorns the nymph. (active — the subject does the scorning)

Nympha spernītur.

The nymph is being scorned. (passive — the subject receives the scorning; who does it? not yet stated)

💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · no wrong answer
You have seen the passive before. Which of these best describes what the passive voice tells you?
📚 PART 2 · THE BIG IDEA 12 min

The passive hides the doer — the agent construction names it

The passive voice removes the doer from the subject position. But Latin often wants to name the doer anyway — especially in narrative, where who does what to whom is the whole story. When Narcissus is scorned, you need to know: scorned by whom? When Echo's voice is heard, by whom is it heard?

Passive alone

Echō spernībātur.
Echo was being scorned.

The doer is missing. Scorned — but by whom?

Passive + agent

Echō ā Narcissō spernībātur.
Echo was being scorned by Narcissus.

The agent construction restores the doer.

That little phrase — ā Narcissō — is the agent construction: the preposition ā/ab plus a noun in the ablative case. It is one of the most useful tools Latin gives you for reading narrative, and by Friday you will spot it on sight. You will study its rules on Day 3, right after you build the future passive on Day 2.

💡 WHY IT MATTERS FOR THIS STORY

Echo's tragedy is a passive one. After Juno's curse, she can only repeat what she hears — her voice audītur, “is heard,” but only as a reflection of someone else's words. The passive voice here is not mere grammar; it is the very shape of her fate.

🏛️ PART 3 · THE STORY AHEAD 10 min

Narcissus and Echo

🏛️ OVID · METAMORPHŌSĒS, BOOK III

Echo is a mountain nymph cursed by the goddess Juno: she may never speak first, and can only repeat the last words she hears. When she falls in love with the beautiful youth Narcissus, she cannot tell him — she can only echo him. He rejects her, and she wastes away until nothing is left but her voice.

Narcissus, who has scorned everyone who loved him, is punished in turn. At a still pool he sees a reflection of astonishing beauty and falls in love — not knowing it is himself. He cannot leave, cannot touch what he loves, and finally dies gazing at his own image. From his name we get the word narcissism.

Notice how much of this story is about things done to people: Echo is cursed, Narcissus is scorned and then punished, a reflection is loved. That is why this module pairs the passive voice with the agent construction — the grammar and the myth are telling the same story.
