⏰ PART 1 · READ THE PASSAGE 16 min

Rīma in Mūrō

After Ovid, Metamorphōsēs IV — adapted for Latin 2. The underlined verbs are future perfect; read aloud, then check yourself against the glossary below.

1Pyramus et Thisbē in Babylonō habitābant. Parentēs eōrum inimīcī erant — inter casās mūrus altus stābat.

2Mūrō erat rīma parva. Per hanc rīmam Pyramus et Thisbē verba sua clam mittēbant.

3Ōlim Pyramus dīxit: “Ō mūre — tū semper inter nōs estās! Sed cum nox advēnerit, ad arborem mōrī prope urbem conveniēmus.”

4Thisbē respondit: “Ita! Cum signum tuum dedērō, venī statim. Cum ego advēnerō, tū quoque veniēs.”

5Thisbē prīma ad arborem properāvit. Sed leaena prope arborem aquam petēbat.

6Thisbē fūgit et pallam suam relīquit. Leaena pallam scīdit.

7Pyramus mox vēnit et pallam vīdit. “Cum Thisbē fūgerit, palla sola manet? Thisbē periit!”

8Pyramus dēspērāvit et sē vulnerāvit. Cum Thisbē reversa erit, Pyramum mortuum inveniet.

GLOSSARY

Babylonō — in Babylon (locative) · inimīcī — enemies, hostile · inter casās — between the houses · clam — secretly · Ō mūre — O wall! (vocative; Ovid addresses the wall) · estās — you stand · leaena — lioness · palla — a long outer garment, shawl · relīquit — left behind · scīdit — tore · sē vulnerāvit — wounded himself · reversa erit — will have returned (fut. perf. of the deponent revertor; glossed for awareness).

📚 PART 2 · OVID'S TALKING WALL 7 min
🏛️ APOSTROPHE · ADDRESSING THE WALL

In the original Latin, Ovid does not just describe the wall — he speaks to it: “Ō nimium dūrae… quid obstās amantibus?” (“O wall too cruel… why do you stand in the lovers' way?”). Speaking to an object as if it could hear is a figure called apostrophe, and it is a signature Ovidian move.

The wall becomes almost a character: an obstacle that grudgingly lets whispers through its crack. It is the one thing that knows both sides of the story — and the lovers even thank it, half-grateful and half-resentful, for the little it allows.

✅ TRY IT · the technique
Ovid speaks directly to the wall (Ō mūre!). This figure of speech is called…

In your own words: how does the future perfect capture the “step-by-step” nature of the lovers' plan? Think it through, then reveal one way to say it.

Their plan is a chain of conditions: when I will have arrived… when you will have given the signal… Each step must be complete before the next can begin, and the future perfect is precisely the tense for “an action finished before another future event.” The grammar mirrors the logic of two people trying to meet against every obstacle.
