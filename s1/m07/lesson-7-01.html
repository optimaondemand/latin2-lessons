📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
In Module 06 you learned the “past of the past.” This week you learn its mirror image: the future that will already be finished.
Two lovers, Pyramus and Thisbē, plan to meet in secret. Their whole plan runs on a chain of steps: when I will have arrived… when you will have given the signal… then we will meet. Each step must be complete before the next can begin. Latin has one tense built exactly for this: the future perfect active. Today there is no paradigm to drill yet — just the big idea and how it fits beside the pluperfect you already know.

⏰ PART 1 · WHERE WE LEFT OFF 6 min

The past of the past

Module 06 gave you the pluperfect active — an action already finished before some other past moment. It is built on the perfect stem plus the endings -eram, -erās, -erat, -erāmus, -erātis, -erant.

Thīsbē fūgerat.

Thisbe had fled. — perfect stem fūg- + pluperfect ending -erat = “had fled,” complete before a later past event.

Amāverant.

They had loved. — perfect stem amāv- + -erant.

Hold onto that idea of a completed action anchored to another event. The future perfect keeps it — and only shifts the whole picture into the future.

📚 PART 2 · THE BIG IDEA 12 min

A future that will already be finished

The future perfect active names an action that will be completed before another future event. English says it with “will have”: By nightfall, Pyramus will have arrived. The arriving is finished; something else then follows.

Pluperfect — M06

advēnerat
he had arrived

Completed before a past event. Looks backward from the past.

Future perfect — this week

advēnerit
he will have arrived

Completed before a future event. Looks forward to a done deal.

Here is the surprise: Latin uses the future perfect far more often than English does. In a “when” or “after” clause about the future, English usually just uses the present tense — but Latin insists on the future perfect.

💡 ONE SENTENCE, TWO LANGUAGES

English: “When you arrive, I will be there.” — Latin: Cum advēneris, ibi erō — literally “When you will have arrived, I will be there.” Latin is being precise: the arriving must be done before the being-there can happen.
