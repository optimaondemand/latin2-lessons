Grammar plus story equals reading. Today the passive and the participles carry the myths that gave this semester its name.

Today you gather those myths back up, read Latin sentences that use your passive and participle grammar, and get ready to write your own transformation. ⏰ PART 1 · READ ALONG 13 min

Sentences you can now read

Read each aloud. Find the subject, spot the passive verb or participle, and sort any “by” phrase into agent or means. The gray line is your parse and translation — try it yourself before you read it.

Daphnē ab Apolline amābātur.

Daphne was being loved by Apollo. — imperfect passive amābātur · ab Apolline = personal agent.

Nympha in laurum mūtāta est.

The nymph was changed into a laurel tree. — perfect passive mūtāta est (PPP + est).

Narcissus imāgine suā dēceptus est.

Narcissus was deceived by his own reflection. — perfect passive dēceptus est · imāgine = means (a thing, plain ablative).

Arachnē, ā deā victa, in arāneam versa est.

Arachne, conquered by the goddess, was turned into a spider. — PPP victa (participial phrase) + perfect passive versa est.

Actaeōn ā canibus suīs necātus est.

Actaeon was killed by his own dogs. — perfect passive necātus est · ā canibus = personal agent (living creatures).

💡 READING TIP

In a transformation myth the passive is everywhere — because the whole point is that something is done to the character: they are changed, deceived, conquered, turned. When you see -tus/-a/-um + est, expect “was ___ed.”

📚 PART 2 · THE MYTHS, GATHERED 14 min
🏛️ OVID, METAMORPHŌSĒS · THE SEMESTER'S MYTHS

Daphne, fleeing Apollo, prays to escape and is changed into a laurel tree. Narcissus, in love with his own reflection, wastes away and becomes the flower that bears his name. Arachne, a weaver proud enough to challenge Minerva, is turned into a spider. Pyramus and Thisbe, forbidden lovers, die by the mulberry tree — whose white fruit turns red with their blood.

Midas, granted that all he touches turn to gold, nearly starves in his own wish. Actaeon, who saw Diana bathing, is changed into a stag and torn apart by his own hounds. Phaethon, unable to steer the sun-chariot of his father, is struck down by Jupiter to save the earth. Io, loved by Jupiter, is changed into a heifer to hide her from Juno. Eight stories, one pattern: a person becomes something else.

⚖️ THE MEANING OF MŪTĀTIŌ

In Ovid, transformation is never random. It reveals something already true — Daphne's flight becomes a tree's stillness, Narcissus's self-love becomes a flower bent over the water, Arachne's pride becomes endless weaving. Change is the mask pulled away. That is the idea at the heart of the whole semester — and the idea your own Transformation Story should carry.

✅ TRY IT · the theme
In Ovid, a transformation usually…

In your own words: pick one myth and explain how the transformation “fits” the character. Think it through, then reveal one example.

One good answer: Narcissus. He could love only his own reflection, so he becomes the narcissus flower — a bloom that leans over still water, forever gazing down. The change makes his inner flaw visible and permanent. (Daphne's flight into stillness, or Arachne's pride into endless weaving, work just as well.)
