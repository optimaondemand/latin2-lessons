★ Putting the PAP to Work — and Eight New Words
Lesson · about 40 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You can build a PAP. Today you learn to use it — and to keep it clearly apart from the PPP.
Three rules govern how a PAP behaves in a sentence: it is active and simultaneous, it agrees with its noun like any adjective, and it translates as “…-ing” or “while / because / when …”. You'll also learn the module's eight new words, which set up tomorrow's reading of Actaeon in the grove.

📝 NOTES ON THESE WORDS

Cornū is a 4th declension neuter noun — its nominative and accusative singular are both cornū. Lacus is also 4th declension (like arcus from M01). Tremō is 3rd conjugation, so its PAP is tremēns, trementis. Conspiciō is 3rd -iō; you will meet its PAP in M12.

📚 PART 2 · THREE RULES FOR USING A PAP 12 min
Rule 1 — Active and simultaneous.

Unlike the PPP (passive, completed), the PAP describes what a noun is doing at the same moment the main verb happens. Actaēōn, in silvā errāns, fontem invēnit — “Actaeon, wandering in the forest, found the spring.” The wandering and the finding overlap in time.

Rule 2 — Agrees with its noun.

Like all adjectives, the PAP agrees in gender, number, and case. Since every nom. sg. is -ns regardless of gender, the noun tells you the gender. vēnātor errāns (nom. sg. m.) · nymphae trementes (nom. pl. f.) · Dianam videntem (acc. sg.).

Rule 3 — Translate as “…-ing” or “while / because / when…”

A PAP on the subject usually becomes an English participial phrase, but it can carry a temporal (while…), causal (because…), or plain descriptive sense. Diana, Actaēōnem conspiciens, īrāta erat — “Diana, catching sight of Actaeon, was angry,” or “When Diana caught sight of Actaeon, she was angry.”

⚖️ STORY CONNECTION

Actaeon's fate is sealed in a single participial phrase. He is not searching for Diana; he is just a hunter wandering away from his companions — the vēnātor errāns — who comes upon her sacred grove with no intent at all. The PAP captures this exactly: an action happening at the very moment everything changes. There is no agency in errāns; there is only motion, and misfortune.

📌 PART 3 · THE TWO PARTICIPIAL TRAPS 5 min

PAP vs. PPP

PAP — always active

videns Dianam
[he,] seeing Diana

He does the seeing; the action happens now, alongside the main verb. Signature: -ns with genitive -ntis.

PPP — always passive

Diana vīsa
Diana having been seen

Diana is the one seen; the action is completed. Signature: a 2-1-2 form, often -tus/-ta/-tum or -sus/-sa/-sum.

A PAP is always active. A PPP is always passive. Never confuse them: -ns / -ntis means PAP; a 2-1-2 adjective form means PPP.

💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · no wrong answer
Which of the three rules do you find hardest to apply right now?

Analyze all three before you look. For each highlighted PAP name (a) its verb, (b) the noun it modifies, and (c) what it tells us is happening at the same time as the main verb:

1. Actaēōn, errāns in silvā, fontem sacrum invēnit.
2. Diana, Actaēōnem videns, īrāta erat.
3. Vēnātor tremēns in aquam cecidit.
1. “Actaeon, wandering in the forest, found the sacred spring.” errāns — PAP of errō (1st conj.), nom. sg., modifying Actaēōn (m.); the wandering is simultaneous with the finding. errāns, errantis.

2. “Diana, seeing Actaeon, was angry.” videns — PAP of videō (2nd conj.), nom. sg., modifying Diana (f.); the seeing is simultaneous with her anger. vidēns, videntis.

3. “The trembling hunter fell into the water.” tremēns — PAP of tremō (3rd conj.), nom. sg., modifying vēnātor (m.); the trembling is simultaneous with the falling. tremēns, trementis — note the 3rd conj. -ē-.
