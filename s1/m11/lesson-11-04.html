⏰ PART 1 · READ THE PASSAGE 18 min

Actaēōn Videt

Read each line aloud. Find the subject and main verb first, then locate every PAP and the noun it modifies. Use the glossary below for the unfamiliar words.

📜 AFTER OVID, METAMORPHŌSĒS III — ADAPTED FOR LATIN 2

1 Actaēōn, vēnātor clārus Thēbārum, per silvam errāns, ā sociīs suīs discessit et sōlus ambulābat.
2 In silvā dēnsā vallem sacram invēnit: ibi Diana, dea vēnātiōnis, cum nymphīs suīs lavābātur.
3 Actaēōn, Dianam in lacū lavantem conspiciens, subitō stetit — timor magnus in corde eius erat.
4 Nymphae, vēnātōrem videntēs, clāmāvērunt et Dianam tegere temptāvērunt.
5 Diana, Actaēōnem conspiciens, īrāta erat: dea, iram suam nōn tenens, aquam in vēnātōrem sparsit.
6 Actaēōn, aquā Dianae tactus, tremēns in undīs lacūs stetit — cornua in capite eius crēscēbant.
7 Vēnātor miser, in cervum mūtātus, in silvam fugit: canes eius, dominum nōn cognōscentēs, eum petīvērunt.
8 Actaēōn, ā canibus suīs petītus, cecidit — sīc vēnātor in praedā factus est.

📝 GLOSSARY

Thēbārum — of Thebes (gen. pl. of Thēbae, -ārum) · sociīs — from his companions (abl. pl. of socius, -ī) · vēnātiōnis — of the hunt (gen. sg. of vēnātiō, -ōnis) · lavantem — bathing (PAP acc. sg. of lavō, modifying Dianam) · subitō — suddenly · stetit — stood, stopped (perf. of stō) · tegere — to cover, conceal (inf. of tegō) · sparsit — sprinkled, scattered (perf. of spargō) · cornua — antlers, horns (nom. pl. of cornū) · crēscēbant — were growing (impf. of crēscō) · canes — dogs, hounds (nom. pl. of canis, canis) · praedā — as prey (abl. of praeda, -ae; in praedā = as the quarry)

📚 PART 2 · CULTURAL READING 7 min
🏛️ ACTAEON IN THE ANCIENT WORLD

The myth of Actaeon was one of the most frequently depicted stories in Greek and Roman art. He appears on hundreds of vase paintings and reliefs, usually at the moment of transformation — partly human, partly stag, surrounded by his own hounds.

The story raised deep questions for ancient audiences: does divine law require intent to be violated? Actaeon did nothing wrong on purpose. His crime was seeing something he was not meant to see. For Ovid this is characteristic: the gods of the Metamorphōsēs are dangerous precisely because their power is disproportionate and their sense of justice impersonal.

✅ TRY IT · comprehension
How does the story end for Actaeon?

In your own words: what makes Actaeon's punishment feel unjust to a modern reader — and why might Ovid have wanted us to feel that? Think it through, then reveal one way to say it.

Actaeon never intended to trespass — he was simply wandering (errāns) and caught sight of Diana by accident. Yet he is punished as harshly as a deliberate offender, transformed and torn apart by his own hounds. Ovid lets the injustice stand: the gods punish the act, not the intent. That disproportion is the point — divine power in the Metamorphōsēs is impersonal and terrifying precisely because it does not weigh a mortal's motives.
