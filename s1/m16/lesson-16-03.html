📚 PART 2 · CARD 3 — AGENT CONSTRUCTION 9 min

Who did it vs. what it was done with

The ablative case expresses both agent and means / instrument in passive constructions — but only the personal agent takes ā/ab.

TypeLatin signalExampleEnglish
Personal Agentā/ab + ablativeā gladiātōre vulnerātuswounded by a gladiator
Means / Instrumentablative alonegladiō vulnerātuswounded by a sword (with a sword)
⚖️ THE RULE

ā/ab + ablative = PERSONAL agent (a being who acts with will). Ablative alone = means or instrument (a thing used to carry out the action). When in doubt, ask: could this thing act independently? A gladiator can; a sword cannot.

📚 PART 3 · CARD 4 — TRANSLATION METHOD 9 min

Four steps for any participial phrase

Use this method every time you meet a participial phrase in a Latin sentence.

1
Find the participle and identify its type (PPP / PAP / FAP). Look for: PPP ends in -tus/-ta/-tum (or 3rd-conj. -sus/-sa/-sum). PAP ends in -ns (gen. -ntis). FAP ends in -tūrus/-tūra/-tūrum.
2
Find the noun the participle modifies. The participle agrees in gender, number, and case with the noun it describes. Match endings to find the pair.
3
Determine the time relationship. PPP = action completed before the main verb. PAP = action happening at the same time. FAP = action intended after the main verb.
4
Render in natural English. PPP: “having been ___ed” or “after being ___ed.” PAP: “while ___ing” or “___ing.” FAP: “about to ___” or “intending to ___.”

📜 TWO NOTES

Redeō is a compound of eō, īre (to go). In the passage it appears as rediit (perfect, 3rd sg.: “she returned”) and rediēbat (imperfect, 3rd sg.: “she was returning”).  ·  Aegyptus is feminine despite its -us ending, because it is a place name (as with many countries in Latin).
