★ Three Ways to Ask — and Five New Words
Lesson · about 38 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A tiny piece of grammar with an outsized payoff — and the five words that open the year's reading.
Latin has three ways to signal that a sentence is a yes/no question, and each one tells you what answer the speaker expects. These particles — nōnne, num, and the enclitic -ne — were held back in Latin 1. They take five minutes to learn and they appear constantly in Ovid, whose characters address, question, and challenge one another all the time. Then you'll meet the module's five vocabulary words — the vocabulary of the course itself.

📚 PART 2 · THE THREE PARTICLES 12 min

nōnne, num, -ne

Each particle carries information about what answer the speaker expects — much more efficient than English, where tone of voice has to do the same work.

1. nōnne — expects YES

Nōnne opens a question and signals that the speaker expects an affirmative answer. English: “Surely…, right?” / “Isn't it true that…?”

Nōnne Ovidius carmina pulchra scrīpsit?
Surely Ovid wrote beautiful poems, didn't he? (Expects: Yes, of course.)
2. num — expects NO

Num opens a question and signals that the speaker expects a negative answer. English: “Surely… not, right?” / “You don't mean to say that…?”

Num dī hominēs amant?
Surely the gods don't love humans, do they? (Expects: No, they don't.)
3. -ne — neutral (enclitic)

The enclitic -ne attaches to the end of the first word (usually the verb or a prominent noun). It signals a neutral yes/no question with no implied expected answer. Translate as “Do…? / Is…? / Are…?”

Legisne tū haec carmina? — Do you read these poems?
Esne Rōmānus? — Are you a Roman?
🧠 MEMORY TRICK

Nōnne contains nōn and expects yes (it introduces a negative that the speaker wants affirmed). Num contains nothing familiar — but its answer is none of the above (i.e., no). The enclitic -ne is the neutral bystander: it just marks the question without taking sides.

📚 PART 3 · FIVE NEW WORDS 6 min

The vocabulary of the course itself

These five words appear in the first module passage (tomorrow's reading) and recur throughout Semester 1. Learn them now.

LatinMeaning
mūtātiō, -ōnisf. — transformation, change
carmen, carminisn. — song, poem
forma, -aef. — shape, form, beauty
dea, -aef. — goddess
aurum, -īn. — gold
💡 WHY THESE FIVE?

Three of them name Ovid's whole project: mūtātiō (change), forma (shape), and carmen (the poem itself). Aurum is included early because the Midas story (Modules 09–10) turns on it — meeting the word now prevents a cold encounter later.
