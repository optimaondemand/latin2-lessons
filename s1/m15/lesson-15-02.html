💡 THE ONE THAT LOOKS ALIKE

Watch the PPP and the FAP: both use -us/-a/-um adjective endings and both are built on the PPP stem (amāt-). The difference is the tiny -ūr- that the FAP inserts before the ending. amātus (PPP, passive, past) vs. amātūrus (FAP, active, future) — one syllable changes everything.

📌 PART 3 · TWO RULES 7 min

Why the table can be trusted

⚖️ RULE 1 · EXACTLY THREE

Latin has no present passive participle (“being loved”) and no perfect active participle (“having loved”). PPP, PAP, and FAP are the complete inventory. Whenever you meet a participial form, it must be one of these three — there is no fourth option to worry about.

⚖️ RULE 2 · EACH HAS ITS MARKER

Every participle wears a signal ending. -ns/-ntis = PAP. -ūrus before the adjective ending = FAP. A plain -us/-a/-um on the PPP stem (no -ūr-) = PPP. Find the marker and you have the participle.

📜 SEEN IN THE STORY

In this week’s reading you will meet mūtāta (PPP: “changed”), canēns (PAP: “playing”), and mūtātūrus (FAP: “about to change”) — all three from the same root, all three distinguished by their markers.
