📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You already own every part. This week you learn to see them as one system.
Over the last several modules you met all three of Latin’s participles one at a time: the PPP in Module 08, the PAP in Module 11, and the FAP in Module 14. No new participial forms are introduced this week. Instead, Module 15 is the synthesis — you learn to see the three participles as a single, complete toolkit that lets Latin say when an action happens, in what voice, and how it relates to the main verb. Today is the big-picture map. The rest of the week fills it in and puts it to work on a story by Ovid.

⏰ PART 1 · WARM-UP 5 min

What you already own

Before we add the map, look at what three earlier modules have already given you. Each of these is a tool you have used before.

amātus, -a, -um

PPP — perfect passive participle: “having been loved.” Passive voice, before the main verb. (Module 08)

amāns, amantis

PAP — present active participle: “loving.” Active voice, at the same time as the main verb. (Module 11)

amātūrus, -a, -um

FAP — future active participle: “about to love.” Active voice, after the main verb. (Module 14)

📚 PART 2 · THE BIG IDEA 14 min

Two axes, three participles

Latin has exactly three participles, and they divide the whole system neatly along two axes: voice (active or passive) and time (before, at the same time as, or after the main verb). Learn the two axes and you can place any participle instantly.

Axis 1 — Voice

Is the noun doing the action (active) or receiving it (passive)?

PAP and FAP are active. PPP is passive.

Axis 2 — Time

When does it happen relative to the main verb?

PPP = before. PAP = same time. FAP = after.

Notice what is not here. Latin has no present passive participle (“being loved”) and no perfect active participle (“having loved”). The three that exist — PPP, PAP, FAP — are the whole inventory. When you see a participial form, it is guaranteed to be one of these three.

💡 WHY THIS MATTERS

A participle lets Latin fold a whole clause into a single adjective. Instead of “the cow, which had been changed,” Latin says vacca mūtāta — “the changed cow.” Three participles means three ways to compress an action into one word, and Ovid uses all three constantly.

🏛️ PREVIEW · IŌ AND ARGUS · OVID, METAMORPHŌSĒS I

This week’s reading is Ovid at his most mischievous: Jupiter falls for the nymph Io, hides his affair under a cloud, and — when his wife Juno grows suspicious — turns Io into a white heifer. Juno sets the hundred-eyed giant Argus to guard her, and Jupiter sends Mercury to lull those hundred eyes to sleep, forever. The episode is packed with all three participles; it reads almost like a grammar exercise Ovid wrote for you.
