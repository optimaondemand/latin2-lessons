📚 PART 1 · VOCABULARY 8 min

Module 12 — eight new words

All eight appear in Thursday's passage. Several are 3rd-declension consonant stems — note the genitive forms carefully, since they show the true stem.

LatinMeaning
canis, canism./f. — dog, hound
fuga, -aef. — flight, escape
pōns, pontism. — bridge
mors, mortisf. — death
lūna, -aef. — moon

📜 WORD NOTES

Lūna belongs to Diana, goddess of the moon and the hunt — Actaeon's destroyer. Canis is a common-gender noun: masculine or feminine depending on the animal. Sanguis hides its stem — sanguis, sanguinis (stem sanguin-). And ululō is onomatopoetic: the Latin word sounds like the howl it names.

📌 PART 2 · THREE WAYS TO TRANSLATE 14 min

The key translation rule

A Latin participial phrase compresses a circumstance that English usually spreads across a whole clause. Three strategies cover almost every PAP phrase. Take one model phrase and watch it render three ways:

StrategyLatinEnglish
SimultaneousActaeōn, fugiēns, timēbat.Actaeon, fleeing, was afraid.
CausalActaeōn, perīculum vidēns, fūgit.Because Actaeon saw the danger, he fled.
TemporalCanēs, cervum vidēntēs, latrābant.When the dogs saw the stag, they were barking.

The same phrase — Canēs, cervum vidēntēs, latrābant — can honestly be rendered all three ways: The dogs, seeing the stag, were barking / Because they saw the stag, the dogs were barking / When the dogs saw the stag, they were barking. Pick the one that reads best in the passage you are translating.

💡 ONE STRATEGY TO SKIP

With a present active participle, the concessive rendering (“although”) is rare and usually wrong. Stick to simultaneous, causal, and temporal.
