⏰ PART 1 · WHAT YOU ALREADY BUILT 8 min

The PAP, from Module 11

Before we add anything new, remember what you can already do. The present active participle is formed from a verb's present stem plus -ns (nominative) / -nt- (other cases), and it declines like a 3rd-declension adjective. It means “___-ing.”

VerbPAP (nom. sg.)Meaning
currō (run)currēnsrunning
videō (see)vidēnsseeing
audiō (hear)audiēnshearing
💭 PRIUS CŌGITĀ · think before you start · no wrong answer
In Module 11 you learned that the PAP agrees with its noun like any adjective. If currēns means “running” and modifies canis (dog), how would you say “I see the running dog” — where “the running dog” is the object (canem, accusative)?
📚 PART 2 · THE BIG IDEA 14 min

One participle, two jobs

Forming the PAP was step one. Now comes the harder, more interesting skill: the PAP does two distinct jobs in Latin, and recognizing which one you are looking at is what lets you translate it accurately.

Job 1 — Attributive

canis currēns
the running dog

The PAP sits right beside its noun and describes it, exactly like an adjective (canis magnus = the big dog).

Job 2 — Circumstantial

Actaeōn, clamōrem audiēns, fūgit.
Actaeon, hearing the shout, fled.

The PAP anchors a whole phrase (set off by commas) that explains how, when, or why the main action happens.

Do not memorize the details yet — that is tomorrow's work. For today, just fix the two categories in your mind: a PAP either modifies a noun (attributive) or anchors a phrase of circumstance (circumstantial). Tomorrow you will learn to tell them apart on sight.

💡 WHY THIS MATTERS FOR OVID

Ovid tells the death of Actaeon almost entirely through cascading circumstantial participial phrases — the transformation, the pursuit, the recognition, the death. Learning to unpack a participial phrase is one of the most important reading skills in all of Latin literature. This week is your first real practice at it.

✅ PART 3 · THE STORY SO FAR 13 min

Where Actaeon stands

🏛️ OVID, METAMORPHŌSĒS III · THE HUNTER ACTAEON

Actaeon was a young hunter of Thebes. One noon, wandering the woods, he stumbled by accident onto a hidden pool where the goddess Diana — goddess of the hunt and the moon — was bathing with her nymphs. No crime, no intention: he simply saw what no mortal was permitted to see.

Diana, furious and ashamed, flung water in his face and cursed him: let him try now to tell that he saw the goddess unclothed — if he can speak at all. In Module 11 you left him at the instant of change: antlers sprouting, his neck lengthening, his skin roughening into hide. A human mind, still frightened and thinking human thoughts, was being sealed inside the body of a stag.

This module finishes the story — and it is not gentle. Actaeon's own hunting dogs, trained by his own hand, will not recognize their master. This week's grammar (the PAP in phrases) is exactly the tool Ovid uses to narrate that pursuit. You will read it on Day 4.

Commit first, then check: in one sentence, what is the difference between the attributive and the circumstantial use of a participle? Say your answer out loud, then reveal.

An attributive PAP sits directly beside a noun and simply describes it, like an adjective (canis currēns = the running dog). A circumstantial PAP is set off (usually by commas) and anchors a whole phrase that explains the circumstances — the how, when, or why — of the main verb (Actaeōn, audiēns, fūgit = Actaeon, hearing, fled).
