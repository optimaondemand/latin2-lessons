📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Yesterday you met the two jobs. Today you learn the rules that tell them apart — and see the full agreement chart.
Three rules run this module. (1) The attributive PAP modifies a noun directly. (2) The circumstantial PAP anchors a verbal phrase. (3) Because the PAP is still a verb, it can take its own direct object inside that phrase. Under all three sits one unbreakable rule you already know: the PAP agrees with its noun in case, number, and gender.

📚 PART 2 · THE THREE RULES 16 min

Rule 1 — the attributive use

In the attributive use, the PAP sits beside its noun exactly as an adjective does. It describes a characteristic action of that noun. Agreement rule: the PAP matches its noun in case, number, and gender.

canis currēns

the running dog (nom. sg. m/f) — currēns works exactly like the adjective magnus in canis magnus.

vēnātor tremēns

the trembling hunter (nom. sg. m) — a pure adjective-equivalent; no comma, no phrase.

Rule 2 — the circumstantial use

In the circumstantial use, the PAP introduces a short verbal phrase that explains the circumstances of the main verb — how, when, or why. The subject of the phrase is the noun the PAP modifies, and the phrase is usually set apart by commas.

Actaeōn, clamōrem nōn audiēns, fūgit.

Actaeon, not hearing the shout, fled. — audiēns anchors a phrase; its subject is Actaeon.

Canēs, cervum vidēntēs, cucurrērunt.

The dogs, seeing the stag, ran. — the phrase explains why they ran.

Rule 3 — the PAP keeps its own object

Because the PAP is a verb form as well as an adjective, it can govern its own accusative direct object. That object belongs to the participle, not to the main verb — do not confuse them.

Actaeōn, Dīanam vidēns, timuit.

Actaeon, seeing Diana, feared. — Dīanam is the object of vidēns, not of timuit.

💡 THE QUICK TEST

Is the PAP fenced off by commas and does it have its own object or adverb? → circumstantial. Is it sitting bare beside a single noun with nothing else attached? → attributive.
