⏰ PART 1 · READ THE PASSAGE 16 min

Actaeōn Cervus

The transformation is complete. Actaeon, now fully a stag, flees the same forest where he once hunted — and his own hounds are closing in. Read each line aloud. The highlighted words are participles; decide the use of each as you go.

AFTER OVID, METAMORPHŌSĒS III · ADAPTED FOR LATIN 2

1Actaeōn, cervus factus, per silvam currēbat; in pedibus eius nunc erant ungulae, in capite cornua.

2Vēnātor, nunc animal tremēns, vōcem suam quaerēbat — sed vōx hūmāna abierat.

3Canēs, ululantēs et cervum olfacientēs, per silvam cucurrērunt.

4Actaeōn, clamōrem canum audiēns, ad pontem fūgit; sed canēs pontem quoque trānsibant.

5Canis prīmus, cervum vidēns, latrāvit et in eum cucurrit.

6Actaeōn, nōmen suum clāmāre nōn potens, in saxīs substitit — fugere iam nōn poterat.

7Canēs, dentibus ācribus ūtentēs, Actaeōnem circumdedērunt; sanguis in terrā fluēbat.

8Dīana, ex monte spectans, rīsit; mors Actaeōnis deam satiāvit.

📜 GLOSSARY

cervus factus — having been made a stag (PPP, review from M08) · ungulae — hooves (nom. pl.) · cornua — horns, antlers (neut. pl.) · vōx hūmāna abierat — his human voice had gone away (pluperfect) · ululantēs — howling (PAP nom. pl. of ululō) · olfacientēs — scenting, sniffing out (PAP nom. pl. of olfaciō) · trānsibant — were crossing over (imperfect of trānseō) · latrāvit — barked (perfect of latrō) · substitit — stopped, stood still (perfect of subsistō) · dentibus ācribus — with sharp teeth (ablative of means) · circumdedērunt — surrounded (perfect of circumdō) · satiāvit — satisfied, pleased (perfect of satiō)

📚 PART 2 · DIANA AS COLD OBSERVER 8 min
🏛️ THE CRUELTY OF DIVINE JUSTICE

One of the most chilling elements of Ovid's Actaeon is the final image: Diana watching from a distance as Actaeon's own dogs tear him apart — and smiling. Ovid does not dwell on guilt or mercy. Diana is satisfied (satiāta).

In her eyes the punishment fits the crime: a hunter is hunted; a man who saw too much is silenced permanently. Actaeon committed no deliberate wrong — he stumbled onto the goddess by accident — yet the penalty is absolute. Ovid uses this detail to comment on the arbitrary, disproportionate cruelty of divine justice, a theme that runs through the whole Metamorphōsēs.

💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · no wrong answer
Which line of the passage struck you most as you read it?

Commit first: translate line 5, Canis prīmus, cervum vidēns, latrāvit et in eum cucurrit, and label the PAP's use. Say your version, then reveal.

Translation: The first dog, seeing the stag, barked and ran at it.  PAP: vidēns is circumstantial — its subject is canis prīmus, its own object is cervum, and the phrase explains why the dog barked. You could equally say “because it saw the stag” (causal) or “when it saw the stag” (temporal).
