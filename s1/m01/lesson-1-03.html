📚 PART 1 · READING STRATEGY 10 min

Find the ending, find the subject

When you meet an unfamiliar verb form, check the ending. If you see -tur, -mur, -minī, or -ntur attached to a 1st conjugation stem, you are looking at a passive form. The subject of that verb receives the action and will be in the nominative case — same as active. What changes is not who the subject is, but what happens to the subject.

Daphne ā multīs laudātur.

Daphne is praised by many. — Daphne (nominative) is the subject; laudātur ends in -tur.

💡 WHO DID IT? · THE “BY” PHRASE

In a passive sentence, the doer often appears in a phrase with ā / ab (“by”): ā multīs (by many), ab Apolline (by Apollo), ā sagittā aureā (by the golden arrow). Use ab before a vowel, ā before a consonant.

📜 WORD NOTES

Plumbum appears in the passage as the adjective plumbea (leaden, made of lead) — and it gives us the chemical symbol Pb for lead and the English word plumber. Arcus is a 4th declension noun; its genitive singular is arcūs. Fugiō is a 3rd conjugation –iō verb, like capiō — note it is not a 1st conjugation verb, so it will never take the endings you learned yesterday.

Reason it through: in Daphne ā sagittā plumbeā vulnerātur, which word is the verb, is it active or passive, and who does the wounding? Say your answer, then reveal.

The verb is vulnerātur — passive (it ends in -tur), 3rd person singular: “is wounded.” Daphne (nominative) is the subject who receives the wound. The doer is ā sagittā plumbeā — “by the leaden arrow.” The whole sentence: “Daphne is wounded by the leaden arrow.”
