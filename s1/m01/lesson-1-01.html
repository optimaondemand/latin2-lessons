Lesson 1.01
★ The Other Side of the Verb — Meeting the Passive Voice
Lesson · about 35 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Latin 1 taught your subjects how to act. Latin 2 begins by teaching them how to be acted upon.
Latin 1 gave you a complete active voice: six tenses, all conjugations, across both numbers. In the active voice, the subject of the sentence performs the action. Latin 2 begins immediately with the other side of the picture — the passive voice, where the subject receives the action rather than performing it. Today there is no paradigm to memorize and nothing to drill — just one big idea, wrapped inside one of the most famous stories in Ovid.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS MODULE YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to state the six present passive personal endings and their meanings, and conjugate any 1st conjugation verb in the present passive.
2
I will be able to distinguish a 1st conjugation present passive form from its active counterpart, and translate sentences that use it.
3
I will be able to read a connected Latin passage from the Apollo and Daphne story, identifying passive forms and their subjects.
⏰ PART 1 · ACTIVE vs. PASSIVE 10 min

Who acts, and who receives?

Every verb has a voice. In the active voice, the subject does the action. In the passive voice, the subject has the action done to it. English shows this with a helper verb (“is,” “was”); Latin, as you will see tomorrow, does it a different way.

Active — the subject acts

Apollo Daphnen amat.
Apollo loves Daphne.

Apollo is the subject, and Apollo does the loving.

Passive — the subject receives

Daphne ab Apolline amātur.
Daphne is loved by Apollo.

Daphne is the subject, but the loving is done to her.

Notice what stays the same: the subject is still in the nominative case. What changes is the direction of the action — and the form of the verb.

📚 PART 2 · WHY THIS STORY 10 min

The grammar mirrors the myth

The passive voice is not optional reading in Latin. It appears on nearly every page of Ovid, Cicero, Caesar, and Virgil. It shapes how Roman writers describe power, fate, and change. And it begins right here, in the story of Apollo and Daphne — a story built entirely around a god who is overwhelmed by love he did not choose.

Apollo is not doing the loving; he is being loved into it by Cupid's arrow. He is acted upon. He receives. He does not initiate. The passive voice is the grammatical shape of his condition.

🏛️ THE MODULE AHEAD

This module introduces the 1st conjugation present passive only. You will add the other conjugations in Modules 02–04, and the imperfect and future passives in Modules 02–03. Everything you meet this week runs on the amā- family you already know from Latin 1 — only the endings are new.

💭 PRIUS CŌGITĀ · think before you start
In the sentence Apollo amat Daphnen (Apollo loves Daphne), Apollo performs the action. If we flip this to “Daphne is loved by Apollo,” Daphne becomes the subject but receives the action. How do you expect Latin to mark this difference?
✅ PART 3 · SPOT THE VOICE 13 min

Active or passive — in meaning

Before you learn the Latin forms, train your ear on the idea. For each English sentence, decide whether the subject is doing the action (active) or receiving it (passive). Work through all five — wrong answers give a hint; use Reset to try again.

✅ TRY IT · which voice?
“Apollo is wounded by the golden arrow.” The subject, Apollo, is…
✅ TRY IT · which voice?
“Cupid throws the arrow.” The subject, Cupid, is…
✅ TRY IT · which voice?
“Daphne is praised by many.” The subject, Daphne, is…
✅ TRY IT · which voice?
“The nymph flees the god.” The subject, the nymph, is…
✅ TRY IT · which voice?
“We are watched by the poet.” The subject, “we,” is…

One to reason through: in the sentence Daphne ab Apolline amātur (“Daphne is loved by Apollo”), who does the loving, and who is the grammatical subject? Say your answer, then reveal.

Apollo does the loving — but he is not the subject. Daphne is the subject (nominative), even though she only receives the action. The doer, Apollo, is tucked into the phrase ab Apolline (“by Apollo”). That is the heart of the passive voice: the receiver, not the doer, is the subject.
