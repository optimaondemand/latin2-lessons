⏰ PART 1 · THE PASSAGE 16 min

Sagitta Aurea

Read the passage aloud. Find each verb; if it ends in -tur, -mur, -minī, or -ntur, it is passive — name its subject. This passage is the basis for the comprehension questions in your Translation Assignment.

After Ovid, Metamorphōsēs I — adapted for Latin 2

1Cupīdō, parvus deus amōris, ōlim in monte Parnāsō sedēbat. Duās sagittās habēbat: ūnam auream, alteram plumbeam.

2Apollo, deus sōlis et arcūs, ad montem vēnit et Cupīdinem superbe rīdēbat: “Puer parvus es — arcus et sagittae sunt arma mea, nōn tua!”

3Cupīdō autem nōn rīdēbat. Sagittam auream cēpit et ad cor Apollinis iacit.

4Statim Apollo ā sagittā aureā vulnerātur: amor magnus in cor eius vēnit.

5Daphne, nympha pulcherrima, fīlia erat Pēnaei deī flūminis. Multī hominēs eam laudābant et amābant.

6Sed Cupīdō sagittam plumbeam ad Daphnen quoque iacit. Daphne ā sagittā plumbeā vulnerātur: virum nūllum amāre vult.

7Apollo Daphnen mox vīdit. Nympha formōsa ab eō laudātur et spectātur — sed Daphne Apollinem nōn amat.

8Daphne ab Apolline fugit. Apollo eam petēbat, sed frustrā.

Glossary:  monte Parnāsō — on Mount Parnassus (sacred to Apollo and the Muses) · ūnam auream, alteram plumbeam — one golden, the other of lead (acc. sg., in apposition to sagittās) · superbe — arrogantly, with pride · arma — weapons (neut. pl. of arma, armōrum) · cēpit — took, seized (perfect active of capiō) · iacit — throws, hurls (present active of iaciō) · deī flūminis — god of the river (genitive, in apposition to Pēnaei) · mox — soon, presently · ab eō — by him (ablative of is, ea, id) · frustrā — in vain, without success
📚 PART 2 · CULTURAL READING 8 min
🏹 CUPID'S TWO ARROWS

This detail is Ovid's own elaboration of an older motif. The gold-tipped arrow inflames love; the lead-tipped arrow repels it. Apollo receives the gold and Daphne the lead — guaranteeing that the more he pursues, the more she flees. It is a story of deliberate, divine cruelty dressed as playfulness.

Ovid's Cupid is not the chubby cherub of later art; he is a vindictive god settling a score with the much-celebrated archer Apollo, who mocked his skill. The passive voice does the storytelling work here: Apollo does not choose to love — he vulnerātur, he is wounded, and love is done to him against his will.

📝 PART 3 · TRANSLATE & PARSE 6 min

In your notes, translate the three sentences below and identify the present passive verb in each, with its person and number. Commit to your answers first, then reveal.

1. Apollo ā Cupīdine amātur.
2. Daphne ā multīs spectātur et laudātur.
3. Nōnne nōs quoque ā poētā laudāmur?
1. Apollo is loved by Cupid. — amātur, 3rd sg; subject is Apollo (nominative).
2. Daphne is watched and praised by many. — spectātur and laudātur, both 3rd sg; subject is Daphne (nominative).
3. Surely we too are praised by the poet, aren't we? — laudāmur, 1st pl; subject is nōs (we); nōnne (from M00) signals an expected “yes.”

✅ TRY IT · parse

In your own words: how does the passive voice fit the meaning of this story? Think it through, then reveal one way to say it.

The story is about a god who does not choose his own fate. Apollo is acted upon — he vulnerātur (is wounded), and love overtakes him against his will. Ovid uses the passive to show that Apollo is not the author of his desire; Cupid is. The grammar and the meaning say the same thing: Apollo receives, he does not initiate.
