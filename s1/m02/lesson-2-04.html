⏰ PART 1 · READ THE PASSAGE 18 min

Daphne Mūtātur

Read the passage aloud. As you go, hunt for every 2nd conjugation passive and every imperfect passive form — they carry the transformation. Use the glossary below for the words you have not formally met.

AFTER OVID, METAMORPHŌSĒS I — ADAPTED FOR LATIN 2
1Apollo Daphnen per agrōs et silvās petebāt. Daphne, virgō pulcherrima, fugere nōn iam potest — fessa erat.
2Tum Daphne ad ripam flūminis Pēnaei vēnit et patrem suum ōrāvit: “Pater, adiuvā mē! Mūtā formam meam et mē servā!”
3Statim precēs virginis ā patre audiēbantur et corpus eius subitō mūtābātur.
4Pedēs eius in radicēs mūtābantur; cortex tenuis corpus iam tenēbātur et tegēbat.
5Crīnēs eius in foliā mūtābantur; rāmī ubi bracchia erant subitō vidēbantur.
6Apollo iam laurum tenēbat. Pectus arboris manibus tangēbat et sub cortice cor movērī sentiēbat.
7Tum Apollo dixit: “Quoniam coniunx mea esse nōn potes, laurus mea erit. Cōma mea, cithara mea, pharetraque meā laurō ornābuntur.”
8Ex illō diē laurus ab Apolline semper amābātur et ā victoribus gestāta est. Sīc flōs et honor Daphnēs ā deō servāntur.
Glossary:  per agrōs et silvās — through the fields and forests · nōn iam — no longer · fessa erat — she was exhausted · ripa — bank (of a river) · ōrāvit — she prayed, begged · precēs — prayers (acc. pl. of prex, precis, f.) · radicēs — roots (acc. pl. of rādīx, f.) · tenuis — thin, fine · tegēbat — was covering (impf. active of tegō, 3rd conj.) · foliā — leaves (acc. pl. of folium, n.) · bracchia — arms (acc. pl. of bracchium, n.) · arboris — of the tree (gen. sg. of arbor, f.) · tangēbat — was touching (impf. active of tangō, 3rd conj.) · movērī — to move, to beat (pres. passive infinitive of moveō) · sentiēbat — was perceiving (impf. active of sentiō, 4th conj.) · quoniam — since, because · cōma — hair, foliage · cithara — lyre · pharetraque — and the quiver · ornābuntur — will be adorned (future passive — introduced in M03) · gestāta est — was worn (perfect passive — introduced in M08) · sīc — thus · honor — honor, glory
💡 WHERE THE PASSIVES ARE

Watch for mūtābātur / mūtābantur (1st conj. imperfect passive — “was/were being changed”), tenēbātur and vidēbantur (2nd conj. imperfect passive), and audiēbantur (imperfect passive of a 4th conj. verb — the endings are the ones you know). The transformation unfolds in stages, which is exactly why Ovid reaches for the imperfect passive.

📚 PART 2 · CULTURAL READING 10 min
🌿 THE LAUREL CROWN · CORŌNA LAUREA

Apollo's declaration in line 7 is not merely poetic — it is an origin story for one of Rome's most powerful symbols. Roman generals who won major victories were granted a triumphus (triumph), and the laurel wreath was central to the procession. Later, Julius Caesar and then Augustus wore laurel crowns as a permanent sign of divine favor and military victory. The tradition passed to every emperor after them — which is why nearly every imperial portrait bust wears the laurel.

The English word laureate (as in “poet laureate” or “Nobel laureate”) comes straight from laurus, through the Latin laureātus (“crowned with laurel”) — honoring those judged supreme in their art, just as Apollo honored the tree that Daphne had become.

📜 WORD TRAIL

moveō gives us move, motion, motive, emotion, promote; teneō gives us tenant, tenacious, retain, contain, tenure. The Latin verbs you drilled this week are hiding inside dozens of English words.

💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · no wrong answer
How did reading the connected passage feel?

Translate on your own: Daphne ā radicībus patris tenētur. Give the English, then name the conjugation, tense, person, and number of the verb. Reveal to check.

“Daphne is held by the roots of her father.” The verb tenētur is 2nd conjugation, present passive, 3rd person singular — stem tenē- + -tur. Daphne (nominative) is the subject; the roots do the holding.
