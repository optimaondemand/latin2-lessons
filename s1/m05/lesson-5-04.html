⏰ PART 1 · READ THE PASSAGE 15 min

Arachnē Superba

Read each line aloud. Find the passive verbs and note whether each is present, imperfect, or future. Use the glossary below for the words you have not formally learned.

After Ovid, Metamorphōsēs VI — adapted for Latin 2
1In parvō oppidō Lydiae Arachnē habitābat. Pater eius lānam tingēbat; fīlia autem tēlās mīrābilēs texēbat.
2Ars Arachnēs in tōtā terrā laudābātur: tēlae eius ā multīs spectābantur et mīrābantur.
3Virginēs et mulierēs ex agrīs et collibus veniēbant ut opus eius vidērent. Digitī Arachnēs semper movēbantur — lāna trahēbātur, colōrēs ōrdinābantur.
4Sed Arachnē superbior fierī coepit. “Ars mea,” dīcēbat, “cum arte Minervae comparārī potest.”
5Haec verba ab omnibus audīta sunt. Nōmen Arachnēs per tōtam Graeciam fertur et laudātur — sed dea nōndum audit.
6Comitēs Arachnēn monēbant: “Cavē, amīca! Dea Minerva ā glōriā suā nōn movēbitur.”
7Arachnē autem nōn monēbātur: glōria sua crēscēbat, superbia crēscēbat.
8Mox fāma Arachnēs ad Olympum ferrī dīcēbātur — et Minerva haec verba audītūra erat.
💡 GLOSSARY

oppidō — in a small town (abl. of oppidum, -ī) · Lydiae — of Lydia (gen.; a region of Asia Minor) · tingēbat — used to dye (impf. active of tingō) · laudābātur — was being praised (1st conj. impf. passive) · mīrābantur — marveled at (deponent, 3pl impf. — active meaning) · ōrdinābantur — were being arranged (1st conj. impf. passive) · comparārī potest — can be compared (present passive infinitive + potest) · fertur — is carried, is reported (3rd conj. present passive of ferō) · cavē — beware! (imperative of caveō) · crēscēbat — was growing (impf. active of crēscō) · ferrī dīcēbātur — was said to be carried (passive + dīcēbātur, reported speech) · audītūra erat — was about to hear (future active periphrastic — a preview; translate “was about to hear”)

📚 PART 2 · THE STORY BEHIND IT 10 min
🏛️ ARACHNE · OVID, METAMORPHOSES VI

Arachne was a mortal girl from Lydia, a region of Asia Minor. She was not born to wealth — her father dyed wool for a living — but her weaving was so fine that people came from the fields and hills just to watch her fingers move across the loom. Her ars (skill) earned her a reputation, her fāma, across the whole land.

But praise turned to pride. Arachne began to boast that her skill could be compared to that of Minerva herself — the goddess of wisdom and of weaving. Her companions warned her: Cavē! — beware. A goddess will not be moved by a mortal's glory. Arachne would not listen.

The passage ends on the edge of the reckoning: the boast is repeated everywhere, carried at last all the way to Olympus — and Minerva is about to hear it. What happens next (Module 06) is the contest, and the transformation that gave the spider its ancient name.

🦙 ETYMOLOGY 3 min
💬 ARACHNID

Arachne's name gave science the word arachnid — the class that includes spiders, scorpions, and mites. At the end of the story (Module 06), Minerva turns Arachne into a spider as punishment for her pride. Because that transformation is the mythological origin of the spider, the ancient Greeks used Arachnē as their very word for spider — and when eighteenth-century biologists named the class of eight-legged arthropods, they reached straight back to Ovid. Every time you say “arachnid,” you are remembering a Latin weaver who dared to compare herself to a goddess.

One more: find the one future passive verb in the passage. Name it and translate it, then reveal.

movēbitur (line 6) — “will be moved.” It is 2nd conjugation future passive: stem movē- + future sign -bi- + passive ending -tur. The companions warn that the goddess will not be moved (nōn movēbitur) by a mortal's glory.
