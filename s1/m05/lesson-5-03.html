💬 WORDS THAT SURVIVED

Several of these live on in English. ars, artis gives us art, artisan, artifact; color is almost unchanged; fāma gives us fame and famous; glōria gives glory and glorious; and texō (to weave) is the root of text, textile, and texture — a written text is literally something “woven” from words.

📌 PART 2 · THE FALSE PASSIVE 9 min

Deponent verbs: passive form, active meaning

A deponent verb looks passive but is active in meaning. mīror is one of them. Its form mīrātur is identical to a 1st conjugation present passive — the -ātur ending is right there — but Latin treats it as fully active in translation.

Looks passive…

mīrātur
ending -ātur, just like amātur

…means active

mīrātur = “he marvels at”
not “he is marveled at”
⚖️ ONE FORM, TWO READINGS

Arachnē artem suam mīrātur. — Arachne marvels at her own skill. (deponent: passive form, active meaning)

Ars Arachnēs ab omnibus mīrātur. — The skill of Arachne is marveled at by all. (true passive — here the subject receives the action)

Latin 3 covers deponents fully — dozens of them. For now, just treat mīror as active whenever you meet it, and let the presence of an agent (ab omnibus) warn you when a genuinely passive reading is intended.

One more, out loud: in Ars Arachnēs ab omnibus mīrātur, should mīrātur be read as active (“marvels at”) or as a true passive (“is marveled at”)? Decide, then reveal.

True passive here — “is marveled at.” The tip-off is the agent ab omnibus (“by all”). When a deponent carries an agent and the subject is clearly receiving the action, read it as a genuine passive. Without an agent, mīrātur keeps its usual active sense.
