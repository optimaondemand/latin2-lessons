⏰ PART 1 · READ THE PASSAGE 15 min

Certāmen Deae et Virginis

Read the passage aloud. As you read, find every underlined verb — each is a pluperfect active — and notice what earlier event it describes. The pluperfect forms tell you the backstory behind each action.

After Ovid, Metamorphōsēs VI — adapted for Latin 2

1Arachnē Lydiae puellae arēs textile mira erat. Iam ante certāmen tēlam pulcherrimam texuerat.
2Minerva, dea sapientiae, in formam anus vēnit et Arachnēn saepe monuerat: “Dēos time, virgō!”
3Sed Arachnē nōn pārēbat: “Nōn dea mē docuerat — ars mea propria est!”
4Certāmen incipit. Minerva in tēlā suā imāginēs deōrum potentium fēcerat.
5Arachnē autem in tēlā suā scelera deōrum texuerat: Iovem, Neptūnum, Apollinem — omnia vēra.
6Minerva opus Arachnēs vīdit. Virgō opus perfectum fēcerat — deae opus superāverat.
7Īrā magnā Minerva tēlam Arachnēs scidit. Arachnē, quae multa et mīra texuerat, cecidit.
8Tum Minerva Arachnēn tetigit et eam mūtāvit: nunc dēformis arānea per omnēs terrās texit.

📜 GLOSSARY

Lydiae puellae — of the Lydian girl (genitive; Lydia is a region in Asia Minor) · arēs textile — the weaving art (nom. sg., subject) · in formam anus — into the form of an old woman (acc. after in, expressing change) · texō, texere, texuī — to weave (perfect stem texu-) · propria — her own, personal · imāginēs — images, likenesses (of imāgō, imāginis) · scelera — crimes, wicked deeds (of scelus, sceleris) · scidit — tore apart (perfect of scindō) · tetigit — touched (perfect of tangō) · per omnēs terrās — through all lands

💡 PLUPERFECT IN THE PASSAGE

Every underlined verb is pluperfect active. Notice how they cluster: Arachnē had woven, Minerva had warned, the maiden had surpassed the goddess. All this was already complete before the contest’s final moment. The pluperfect gives narrative depth — it tells you the history that makes the present terrible.

📚 PART 2 · CULTURAL READING 12 min
🏛️ OVID’S SUBVERSIVE ARACHNĒ

Arachnē was a mortal weaver from Lydia so skilled that people said she must have been taught by Minerva herself — the goddess of wisdom and craft. Arachnē refused the compliment: her art, she said, was her own. She challenged the goddess to a contest.

Minerva wove images of the gods in their glory and power. Arachnē wove something else entirely: the gods’ crimes — Jupiter seizing Europa, Neptune, Apollo pursuing mortals against their will. Her tapestry was true, and it was technically perfect. It even surpassed the goddess’s own work.

Minerva, enraged, tore the tapestry apart and struck Arachnē. In her despair Arachnē tried to hang herself; Minerva, in a final act, transformed her into a spider — condemned to weave forever. That is why, Ovid tells us, spiders still spin their webs in every land.

⚖️ WHY IT UNSETTLES

This is one of Ovid’s most quietly subversive stories. The mortal’s art was better, her subject matter was true, and she was punished for both. Ovid never says Minerva was wrong to be angry — he simply tells you what Arachnē had woven, and lets you decide. The pluperfect is doing quiet work here: it lays out everything Arachnē had already done before the goddess acted, so that the punishment lands on a full record of the truth.

✅ PART 3 · CHECK YOUR READING 13 min

Five checks — some test the story, some test your Latin reading. Take them slowly; the point is to prove you can both read the sentences and follow the tale.

✅ TRY IT · comprehension

✅ TRY IT · translate

✅ TRY IT · the subject matter
What did Arachnē weave into her own tapestry?
✅ TRY IT · find the pluperfect
In line 6, which verb is pluperfect active meaning “had surpassed”?
✅ TRY IT · the ending
Into what did Minerva finally transform Arachnē?

In your own words: why is this myth unsettling, and how does the pluperfect help Ovid make his point? Think it through, then reveal one way to say it.

Arachnē was punished even though her art was better and her subject — the gods’ misdeeds — was true. Ovid leaves the judgment to us. The pluperfect verbs (texuerat, monuerat, superāverat) lay out everything that had already happened — the skill, the warnings, the victory — so the transformation lands against a complete record of what Arachnē had done and been.
