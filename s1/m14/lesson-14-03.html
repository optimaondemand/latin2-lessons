Lesson 14.03
★ PPP, PAP, FAP — Telling Them Apart
Lesson · about 40 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You now have all three participles. Today you learn to see the whole system at once — and to spot which one is which.
Latin has exactly three participles, and together they cover every time relationship a noun can have to the main verb: before (PPP), during (PAP), after / still to come (FAP). Today you set them side by side, learn the tell-tale endings that identify each, meet a preview construction (FAP + esse), and study the eight new words you’ll need for tomorrow’s reading.

📚 PART 1 · THE THREE-PARTICIPLE TABLE 10 min

All three from one verb

Here are all three participles built from amō. Notice how each ending is distinct — the ending alone tells you which participle you are looking at.

ParticipleForm (from amō)VoiceTime vs. main verbMeaning
PPPamātus, -a, -umPassivePast — before main verb“having been loved”
PAPamāns, amantisActivePresent — simultaneous“loving”
FAPamātūrus, -a, -umActiveFuture — after / next“about to love”

The same three come from videō: vīsus (having been seen — PPP), videns (seeing — PAP), visūrus (about to see — FAP). Latin gives you a full timeline of action: what was done before, what is happening now, and what is coming next.

💡 THE ENDINGS ARE YOUR SIGNPOSTS

— Ends in -us / -a / -um on the supine stem, passive meaning → PPP.
— Ends in -ns (stem -nt-, e.g. -ntēs), active meaning → PAP.
— Ends in -ūrus / -ūra / -ūrum → FAP, always.

📌 PART 2 · A PREVIEW: THE ACTIVE PERIPHRASTIC 6 min

FAP + esse

The FAP is often combined with a form of esse to form the active periphrastic conjugation — a construction expressing imminent or intended action. You don’t need to master this yet, but you should recognize it when you see it.

Amātūrus est.

He is about to love / he intends to love. — FAP agrees with the subject; est supplies the tense.

Casūra erat.

She was about to fall. — casūra (fem.) agrees with a feminine subject; erat makes it past.

Eight new words

All eight appear in tomorrow’s Latin passage. Study them now.

💬 NOTES ON THESE WORDS

Cadō has an irregular perfect (cecidī) and its FAP is casūrus (from the supine stem cās-). Pīnus is 2nd declension despite ending in -us, and is feminine — tree names are typically feminine in Latin. Convertō appears in the passage describing the transformation of Phaethon’s sisters.

Which of the three participles do you find hardest to spot so far?

Commit first, then check: identify the participle in Sorōrēs, in pīnōs mūtātūrae, ad flūmen stābant — name its type, voice, and time, and say why. Then reveal.

FAP. mūtātūrae = FAP nom. pl. f. of mūtō (supine stem mūtāt- + -ūrae). Active voice, future time. It is feminine plural to agree with sorōrēs — “the sisters, about to be changed into pine trees.” The -ūr- is the giveaway.
