⏰ PART 1 · THE PASSAGE 16 min

Phaethōn Cadit

Phaethon has taken the reins of the sun’s chariot; the horses immediately know they carry a weaker hand. Read aloud. Find every participle (bolded) and decide whether it is PPP, PAP, or FAP. Note especially how the FAP charges the narrative with impending catastrophe. The glossary follows.

AFTER OVID, METAMORPHŌSĒS II · ADAPTED FOR LATIN 2
1Phaethōn, frēna accepta, equōs ad caelum altum agere temptāvit; sed equī, sentiēntēs manum levem, statim ex viā sōlis cucurrērunt.
2Phaethōn, terram videns longē sub pedibus, timuit; frēna nōn retenta, equī furōre magnō in terrās descendēbant.
3Flammae ex currū cadentēs terram incendēbant; silvae ardēbant, flūmina siccābantur, terrae ipsae ūstae erant.
4Iuppiter, terrās spectāns, dolōre et īrā commōtus est; iam fulmen, iactūrus, in manū tenēbat.
5Phaethōn, casūrus, nōmina deōrum frustrā clāmāvit; Iuppiter fulmen magnum ad eum iēcit.
6Phaethōn, fulmine percussus, ē currū cecidit; per caelum longum velut stella cadēns in flūmen Ēridanum lāpsus est.
7Sorōrēs Phaethontis, frātrem quaerēntēs, ad rīpam Ēridanī vēnērunt et ibi diū lacrimābant.
8Sorōrēs, in pīnōs mūtātūrae, ad flūmen stābant; paulātim cortex circum corpora eārum crēscēbat, et in arborēs conversae sunt.
📜 GLOSSARY

frēna accepta — PPP of accipiō, the reins having been received · sentiēntēs — PAP of sentiō, sensing, feeling · manum levem — a light hand (acc. sg.) · nōn retenta — PPP of retineō, not held back, not restrained · cadentēs — PAP of cadō, falling · ūstae — PPP of ūrō, burned, scorched · iactūrus — FAP of iaciō, about to hurl · casūrus — FAP of cadō, about to fall · fulmine percussus — PPP of percutiō, struck by the thunderbolt · cadēns — PAP of cadō, falling (like a falling star) · Ēridanum — the Eridanus river (acc. sg.) · lāpsus est — glided, fell (perfect of lābor, deponent) · quaerēntēs — PAP of quaerō, seeking, looking for · mūtātūrae — FAP nom. pl. f. of mūtō, about to be changed · cortex — bark (of a tree) · crēscēbat — was growing (imperfect of crēscō) · conversae sunt — were transformed (perfect passive of convertō)

📚 PART 2 · CULTURAL READING 9 min
🏛️ THE SISTERS BECOME POPLARS

Ovid tells us that Phaethon’s sisters (the Heliades, daughters of Helios) wept so long and so bitterly on the bank of the Eridanus river that the gods transformed them into poplar trees — and their tears hardened into amber. This is one of Ovid’s many aetiological myths (myths that explain the origin of something in the natural world). Amber was a precious commodity in the ancient world, traded up through central Europe from the Baltic coast; the Romans treasured it. Ovid’s myth gives it a heartbreaking origin: the fossilized tears of grieving sisters.

⚖️ WHY THE FAP FITS THIS STORY

The FAP is Ovid’s favorite way to express fate. As the horses bolt and the chariot plunges, Phaethon is casūrus — about to fall. The single word carries the weight of his entire doom. The FAP does not just describe a future action; it charges the moment with dramatic inevitability. This is why Latin participles are so powerful: they compress a whole tragic destiny into one adjective.

💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · no wrong answer
How did the reading feel — could you track the participles as you went?

Commit first, then check: in your notes, translate line 4 — Iuppiter, terrās spectāns, dolōre et īrā commōtus est; iam fulmen, iactūrus, in manū tenēbat. Identify both participles by type. Then reveal.

“Jupiter, watching the lands, was moved by grief and anger; already, about to hurl it, he held the thunderbolt in his hand.” — spectāns = PAP (active, simultaneous: watching); iactūrus = FAP (active, future: about to hurl). The two participles set the scene and load the next instant with menace.
